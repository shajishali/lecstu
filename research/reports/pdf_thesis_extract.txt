ABSTRACT 
University students often need to find information about their timetables, staff members, appoint-
ments, facilities, and directions from different systems or by asking people directly. This process can 
be taking a lot of time and makes it difficult for students to handle their daily university activities 
efficiently. This project developed an AI -powered website called LECSTU, which brings these ser-
vices together on a single platform. L ECSTU provides role -based information and allows users to 
view their personal timetables, check the availability of lecturers and lecture halls, book appointments, 
receive real-time notifications, navigate inside university buildings using QR codes, interact with the 
system through English voice commands, and use a specialized chatbot to obtain university -related 
information. 
The study used a Design Science Research approach in which the platform was used as both the re-
search output and the testing platform. The ASR study evaluated word error rate and latency for local 
Whisper configurations and a Google cloud baseline on English academic utterances. The chatbot 
study used five-fold cross-validation and a separate test set to assess  intent classification and entity 
extraction. Translation performance was assessed using BLEU, semantic similarity, latency, and hu-
man adequacy/fluency over 100 academic sentence sets. Whisper medium achieved the lowest mean 
word error rate for English (0.0410), compared with 0.0806 for Google Speech-to-Text, but required 
substantially greater mean latency (14,018.39 ms versus 3,331.13 ms). The Rasa chatbot received 
cross-validated intent F1 of 0.904 and entity F1 of 0.953, supporting the chatbot hypothesis. The Mar-
ian-based transla tion engine completed 1,800 automated translations without runtime errors and 
achieved a mean human overall score of 3.76/5.0, but Tamil and Sinhala directions-especially Sinhala-
to-Tamil-remained unreliable in practice and are not claimed as completed contributions. 
The results indicate that local AI services can be integrated local AI services with a practical university 
platform deployed at https://lecstu.com. The main contribution is an integrated and reproducible re-
search artifact that links chatbot an d voice features  to live timetable, appointment, availability, and 
navigation services, instead of operating only as a separate chatbot  or a language-only prototype. A 
student usability study (n = 20, July 2026) reported mean SUS 60.53 (SD 14.45), significantly below 
the industry benchmark of 68 (t(18) = −2.26). Core features (timetable, halls, appointments) scored 
4.7–4.9/5; voice and Tamil/Sinhala translation scored ~3.4 –3.7/5, with timetable ease rated higher 
than voice ease (mean difference 1.39, paired t(17) = 4.57). All twenty participants would recommend 
the platform. The RO -4 validation is done using user satisfaction, SUS and perceived efficiency 
measures. Future development will focus on Tamil and Sinhala speech recognition and translation.

Chapter 1: Introduction 
1.1 Background 
Universities depend on timely access to academic and administrative information.  A student might 
need to find where a lecture hall is, whether a lecturer is free, find a free room, look at a timetable 
change or book an appointment. Information is shared with students, staff, and other members of the 
university community through web pages, notice boards, messaging groups, help desks, and separate 
information systems. Because these sources are fragmented, rou tine tasks require more steps, and 
users may find it difficult to locate information consistently. 
Standard interfaces also assume that a user can type in a correct query, navigate the menu, and read 
the text. Utilizing voice input and conversational interactions can help to reduce some of these barriers 
when connected to live institutional data such as timetables, appointments, availability, notifications, 
and locations. An effective academic assistant must do more than just answer static frequently asked 
questions. 
Based on earlier application of campus assistants and university chatbots, mobile and conversational 
access helps in improving the availability of student services [1], [2]. It has been shown that it is 
technically feasible to have a voice-interactive student support [4].  But, many of the works evaluate 
an isolated chatbot or kiosk at a location. It does not investigate how one deployable, testable platform 
can be configured for speech recognition, domain-specific intent recognition, academic logistics and 
indoor navigation. 
Through LECSTU, this research work addresses that integration gap. The system is a responsive web-
based platform for students, lecturers and administrators, backed by an Express/PostgreSQL 
application backend and AI microservices in Python, already in production at https://lecstu.com.  This 
offers authentication, RBAC, personalized timetables, staff/facility availability, appointment 
management & notifications, campus maps, graph-based indoor routing with QR positioning, English 
voice to text, conversational academic assistance through Rasa custom actions, and an optional 
translation layer for Tamil and Sinhala UI text. The main evaluation of the platform is done in English; 
Tamil and Sinhala speech and translation infrastructu re were implemented, but quality level did not 
reach acceptable levels during this project cycle.

1.2 Research Problem 
Students and faculty do not have one platform to manage their daily academic activities. Current 
systems have functions mainly use text and are not linked to the universities current information. This 
can cause users to waste time looking for information or depend on staff to handle repeated requests. 
The technologies like Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) chatbot and machine translation are 
available as products. However, it is unclear if they are suitable, for handling queries.  The ASRs 
ability to understand speech correctly can vary depending on the language and way of speaking. 
Systems that identify what the user wants can get confused with tasks that have meanings. Translations 
can be fluent but it changes the academic meaning. Accurate models can still be difficult to use in 
real-life situations if their response time is slow. The research problem is both an engineering and an 
empirical problem. 
1. How can these components be integrated into one platform that can be used in real -life situations? 
2. How well do they support university tasks in the implemented setting when it comes to handling 
activities? 
The goal is to find out how to combine these technologies into a platform that can effectively handle 
academic queries and support university tasks. 
1.3 Research Aim 
Objective of research is to design, develop and evaluate an AI based academic platform to improve 
accessibility of faculty details and student services in university system. 
1.4 Research Objectives 
RO-1: Development and evaluation of an ASR system that supports academic voice queries in 
English, with Tamil and Sinhala being left for future research. 
RO-2: Development and trai ning of a domain -specific NLP chatbot which can perform academic 
intent recognition and entity extraction. 
RO-3: Implementation and evaluation of the machine translation component using local MarianMT 
for translating academic material, while Tamil/Sinhala translation will be tested in the future. 
RO-4: Evaluation of the impact of AI integration on performance, satisfaction, and accessibility using 
a usability study.

1.5 Motivation 
The reason for doing this research is that it helps people in a way and it is also good for academics. 
 
Practical reason 
At the Faculty of Computing and Technology University of Kelaniya students and sta ff need to use 
timetables, book halls find out when lecturers are available and navigate buildings every day. They 
have to look for this information in different places like notice boards timetables in PDF format 
messaging groups help desks and different w eb pages. This is a waste of time. People have to ask 
administrative staff for help a lot. It is also hard for people to get this information if they cannot type 
or use menus easily like when they're walking between lecture halls or carrying things around the 
campus. If we had one platform that lets people use their voice ask questions and navigate inside the 
university and get real time information it would make things a lot easier. If we make this system 
work as a web application that people can actually use it will be really useful for the university. 
 
Academic reason 
Other studies have looked at parts of this problem like campus apps, university chatbots, speech 
recognition and indoor navigation. They have not often put all these things together in one system that 
people can use and that is connected to real university services. This project tries to fill that gap by 
using Design Science Research. The system we made called LECSTU is the system and also a test to 
see how it works. This study also helps us understand the trade -offs, between how accurate the 
system's how long it takes to work, privacy and how hard it is to set up especially for universities. We 
know that adding Tamil and Sinhala languages is important based on research that has been done on 
languages that are not well supported. For now, we focused on what we could do reliably: using 
English voice and chatbot organizing academic things and navigating inside buildings using graphs.

1.7 Scope 
The system has three roles: the student, the lecturer and the administrator. It covers things like user 
profiles, master timetables and personalized timetables. It also includes hall and lecturer availability, 
appointments and real -time notifications. You can even get floor -plan maps and indoor navigation 
with QR positioning. The system has voice input and you can interact with it using a chatbot. If you 
want you can also get the interface translated into Tamil or Sinhala. 
The main language used in the system is English. We looked at how the system works in terms of 
things like voice recognition quality and how long it takes to get a response. We also checked how 
well the system can understand what you are saying and extract the information. We even tested how 
well the system can translate English into Tamil and Sinhala. We gave a questionnaire to 20 students 
to see how easy the system is to use. 
This system is not meant to replace all the systems that universities use. It is a test system that we are 
using to see how well it works. We only have benchmarks for English so we could not test the voice 
recognition for Tamil and Sinhala. W e do have a system in place to translate languages. The quality 
of the translation especially from Sinhala to Tamil is not good enough to use in a university setting. 
We were able to get 20 students to fill out our questionnaire, which's what we were hopin g for. We 
are being upfront about the limitations of our system, then trying to make it seem like it can do more 
than it can.

1.8 Significance and Contributions 
Contributions from the research include: 
1.A platform to study how AI works with data on schedules, appointments, facilities and directions. 
2.A way to test speech recognition for academic questions, which can also be adapted for Tamil and 
Sinhala languages. 
3.A model that understands 11 types of requests and six types of information with the ability to ac-
cess live data. 
4.A navigation system that uses AI and graphs to find the route indoors with features, like QR code 
tracking and voice directions. 
5.A method to evaluate translations using both computer scores and human feedback which shows 
that Tamil and Sinhala language support needs work. 
6.A real-life example of balancing accuracy, speed, privacy and complexity when using AI in a uni-
versity setting. 
1.9 The Layout of the Thesis 
This thesis presents the development, evaluation and implications of LECSTU, a system that helps 
students and faculty. LECSTU is a platform that brings together features. These features include 
timetables, staff and hall availability, appointments and real -time notifications. It also offers indoor 
navigation, English voice input and conversational academic assistance. All these features are 
available in one web application. The thesis is divided into seven chapters. There are also references 
and appendices. Each chapter deals with a part of the research on LECSTU. The goal is to show how 
LECSTU works and what it can do. LECSTU aims to make academic life easier for students and 
faculty. It uses AI to provide a user-friendly experience. The development and evaluation of LECSTU 
are parts of this thesis. The implications of using  LECSTU are also discussed. LECSTU is designed 
to be a tool for the academic community. It provides benefits, including easier access to information 
and better communication. The thesis explains how LECSTU achieves these goals.

motivation for the project at the Faculty of Computing and Technology, University  of Kelaniya, and

Chapter 2: Literature Review 
2.1 Introduction 
This chapter lays out the concept and empirical foundation of the research. It covers digital student 
support, conversational agents, speech recognition, machine translation, navigation, and responsible 
AI. 
2.2 Digital Student Support and Campus Applications 
 
Still, even though access to services through mobile and web applications guarante es uninterrupted 
availability of the services, availability of the functions alone does not mean their utilization. 
Development of an application for a student interactive assistant with chatbot functions during the 
pandemic period by Sweidan serves as an example of utilizing chatbot functions together with other 
mobile ones [1]. According to the recently conducted study, most of the university chatbots serve only 
to guide through Frequently Asked Questions, admission and basic service processes [2]. They o ffer 
increased availability but do not cover transactional and timing components of the information 
provided. 
Operational efficiency of an application within the educational institution requires non-fragmentation. 
An answer to the question regarding timeta bles should be based on existing information related to a 
particular user. Answers to appointment questions require knowledge of lecturer availability and 
booking policy. Thus, LECSTU regards chatbot capabilities as an access tool to academic services. 
2.3 Conversational Agents in Higher Education 
Rule-based chatbots have predictable responses but are constrained by manually enumerated phrases 
and dialogue flow paths. The NLU pipeline using data -driven methods classifies user’s intent and 
extracts entities like a lecturer, a course, day, time, building, or hall. Rasa is chosen for its support of 
configurable on -premises NLU and dialogue components, domain data, custom actions, and 
controlled integration with private APIs [5]. This is important when the query  contains institution-
specific information that is not supposed to be fed to a generic cloud language model. 
Academic intents can be very similar to one another. “Can I meet Dr. Silva tomorrow?” could mean 
checking for availability or making an appointment. “Where is Main Hall?” could mean a building, a 
===== PAGE 22 =====
 
                                                                                                                                         21 
 
hall, or a path. Thus, the performance of a real evaluation needs to report confusion matrix and per -
class measures, and not just accuracy. Five -fold cross -validation will give a more conservative 
measure than one favourable held-out split. 
2.4 Automatic Speech Recognition for Voice Access 
The transcription of ASR is done into text which will be sent to the NLU module. The whisper has 
large-scale training using multilingual and multitasking supervision with m odels having different 
accuracies and computations in different sizes [3]. The ASR done in the cloud gives the managed 
environment but also comes with the dependency of internet, costs, and privacy problems. 
WER for ASR is the standard measure of performance and is defined by

2.5 Machine Translation for Academic Content 
 
Machine translation could be useful for UI localization and notification of researchers in academia. 
Local training of transformer models could reduce dependency on cloud providers; however, managed 
machine translation servic es have better infrastructure and much more training data. Neural 
translation models for particular language pairs are offered by MarianMT, whereas multilingual 
sequence-to-sequence models for several tasks are offered by mBART [6], [7]. BLEU score measures 
the intersection of n -grams between translated text and reference; however, it does not consider 
multiple variations of the same sentence, especially if there are short sentences and morphologically 
rich languages. This is the reason why this research wi ll employ BLEU score with multilingual 
embedding similarity, latency, and human adequacy and fluency scores. In this research, English was

the primary language of the user interface. Translation from Tamil and Sinhala was conducted 
experimentally but not generated. 
2.6 Campus and Indoor Navigation 
Outdoor campus maps are inadequate when a student moves into a building with multiple floors. The 
indoor navigation process needs a mapping of room structures, connecting corridors, stairways,  
elevators, and connectors.Graph-based routing models the places as nodes with weighted edges for 
links that could be traversed. While A* employs a heuristic function to prefer good paths, Dijkstra’s 
algorithm is a non -heuristic approach for computing shortest path [8], [9]. Q R codes allow cost -
effective indoor location due to the ability to map the user position to a particular graph node through 
scanning, without requiring special wireless facilities. The LECSTU solution incorporates floor plans 
images and a navigation graph reviewed by the administrator. OCR and image processing techniques 
aid in populating room and corridor nodes, but at the same time, route calculation at runtime employs 
the persisted graph. 
2.7 Accessibility, Privacy, and Responsible AI 
Voice input could improve the accessibility of the application for people who find it difficult to use 
typing or complex menus, but voice inputs are sensitive biometric information. Processing in the 
cloud exposes speech or text to third parties, so responsible implementatio n will involve controls on 
recording, consent for research recordings, data minimization, access controls, and retention period. 
Authentication, authorization, password hashing, rate limiting, validation, and logging should also be 
provided as the applicat ion deals with schedule and appointments. Multilingual capability does not 
fully address accessibility; responsive design, keyboard access, descriptive labelling, contrast ratio, 
error correction, and compatibility with assistive technologies should be tes ted against the WCAG 
recommendations [10].

Chapter 3: Methodology 
3.1 Research Design 
Design Science Research is used in this research since the prima ry activity of it is the construction 
and assessment of an information system artifact. This process includes problem identification, 
objectives definition, artefact design and development, artifact demonstration using university tasks, 
artifact components' assessment, and result communication. ASR, NLU, and translation are assessed 
via quantitative experiments.  A mixed -method approach to usability evaluation is planned, which 
includes task -based assessment,  questionnaire-based assessment, and qualitative f eedback. The 
independent variables are ASR engine/model, language, translation engine, language direction, and 
interaction mode. The dependent variables are WER, CER, latency, intent precision/recall/F1, entity 
precision/recall/F1, BLEU, semantic similarit y, task completion time, task success, SUS score, and 
participant feedback.

3.5 AI Component Implementation 
3.5.1 ASR Pipeline 
Audio is accepted through the voice interface, normalized, and sent to the ASR service. The service 
provides interchangeable Whisper, Google, Azure, and fine -tuned Whisp er engines. Local pre-
processing utilities support loading, resampling, and noise reduction. A transcription and timing 
metadata are returned to the client or forwarded to the chatbot. 
The experiment compared Whisper tiny, base, small, medium, a fine-tuned tiny model, and Google’s 
English configuration in the available valid run. Whisper medium provides the strongest tested 
English WER but incurs the highest latency. Engine selection can therefore be based on deployment 
constraints rather than one metric. T amil and Sinhala benchmark audio were prepared, but valid 
Tamil/Sinhala transcriptions were not produced in the recorded run because of a missing FFmpeg 
dependency; finetuning datasets for Tamil (IISc -MILE SLR127) and Sinhala (SLR52) were 
documented for future work [11], [12].

3.5.2 Rasa Chatbot 
The chatbot defines 11 intents: `ask_timetable`, `ask_hall_availability`, `ask_lecturer_availability`, 
`book_appointment`, `cancel_appointment`, `ask_directions`, `ask_office_location`, `greeting`, 
`goodbye`, `fallback`, and `out_of_scope`. There are six entity types related to course, lecturer, hall, 
day, time, and building. NLU processing utilizes a whitespace tokenizer, regular expressions and 
lexico-syntactic features, word/character count vector features, and a c lassifier named DIET, which 
is trained for 100 epochs. The dialogue policy includes memorization, rules, handling of unexpected 
intents, and TED.  
Custom actions link the identified intent and entities to the current platform information. For instance, 
the request about the timetable is answered based on the data of the authenticated user, and the request 
about directions triggers map search and path calculation. Therefore, there is no need to store volatile 
timetable/availability information in the training dialogues.

3.5.3 Translation Pipeline 
Translation service provides access to local MarianMT, mBART -50, and optional Google/Azure 
adapters. Benchmark runs on the same corpus, metric function, logging format, and repeats for each 
engine. Pivot translation is employed in case of absence of direct trans lation model, which might 
increase error rate and latency.  The system records candidate text, reference, BLEU, embedding 
similarity, latency, language pair, category, engine, and run number. In production use, the platform 
defaults to English; Tamil and Si nhala UI translation is available but was not relied upon as a 
completed feature because output quality was inconsistent, especially for Sinhala-to-Tamil.

3.5.4 Indoor Navigation 
Floor plan images are uploaded by administrators and AI detection results for rooms/doorway are 
reviewed by administrators. Approved nodes and edges are added to the PostgreSQL database. The 
route algorithm used at runtime is A*, and Dijkstra serves as a fallback option. Cross-floor routes are 
based on staircases or lifts, while building connectors allow fo r cross-building routes. A QR code is 
used to map the user to an existing node and the platform outputs route geometry, distance, ETA, and 
instructions. Voice commands such as "Take me to the cafeteria" are processed using the same 
mechanism.

export function astar( 
  nodes: PathfindingNode[], 
  edges: PathfindingEdge[], 
  startId: string, 
  goalId: string 
): string[] | null { 
  const byId = new Map(nodes.map((n) => [n.id, n])); 
  if (!byId.has(startId) || !byId.has(goalId)) return null; 
 
  const adj = buildAdjacency(nodes, edges); 
  const goal = byId.get(goalId)!; 
  const open = new Set<string>([startId]); 
  const cameFrom = new Map<string, string | null>(); 
  const gScore = new Map<string, number>([[startId, 0]]); 
  const fScore = new Map<string, number>(); 
  const start = byId.get(startId)!; 
  fScore.set(startId, euclidean(start.x, start.y, goal.x, goal.y)); 
 
  while (open.size > 0) { 
    let current: string | null = null; 
    let bestF = Infinity; 
    for (const id of open) { 
      const f = fScore.get(id) ?? Infinity; 
      if (f < bestF) { bestF = f; current = id; } 
    } 
    if (!current) break; 
    if (current === goalId) return reconstructPath(cameFrom, goalId);

    open.delete(current); 
    const gCur = gScore.get(current) ?? Infinity; 
 
    for (const neighbor of adj.get(current) || []) { 
      const tentative = gCur + neighbor.weight; 
      if (tentative >= (gScore.get(neighbor.nodeId) ?? Infinity)) continue; 
      cameFrom.set(neighbor.nodeId, current); 
      gScore.set(neighbor.nodeId, tentative); 
      const nb = byId.get(neighbor.nodeId)!;

Nodes carry floor -plan coordinates (`x`, `y` as percentages). `buildAdjacency` assigns each edge a 
non-negative weight -either an administrator -set value or Euclidean distance plus optional floor -
transition and vertical-connector penalties. The algorithm maintains `gScore` (best known cost from 
the start) and `fScore(n) = gScore(n) + h(n)`, where h(n) is the straight -line Euclidean distance from 
node n to the goal in the same coordinate space. 
Complexity: For a graph with V nodes and E edges, each iteration scans the open set linearly (`O(V)`), 
and each edge relaxation is `O(1)`, giving O(V² + E) in the worst case for this implementation. With 
the LECSTU graph being sparse and made up of dozens or even hundreds of nodes on each floor, the 
running time of the algorithm is close to z ero on the server. The algorithm using a priority queue 
would have the worst case time complexity of O(E log V), but in our particular case the realization of 
this approach was not needed at all. 
The function h which is used for checking the admissibility of the heuristic is admissible only when 
the cost of all edges is larger than or equal to the geometrical distance between the endpoints of these 
edges in the floor plan. This condition is always fulfilled for the default LECSTU edge costs since the 
distances through corridors can never be shorter than the distance on the floor plan. Any penalties due 
to floor change or different stairwells add to the cost and do not make the function h inadmissible 
though they can make it non -optimal for multi-floor routes. If the algorithm A fails to find the goal 
state (for instance because of disconnection of the subgraph or the edge costs), the function find 
Shortest Path uses Dijkstra’s algorithm which always finds the shortest path if it exists.

what we did. Try to do it again. This includes things, like versioned scripts, manifests, configuration

- Multi-run variance: report SD across the three seed runs. 
Acceptance for extending H1 to Tamil/Sinhala requires valid FFmpeg -backed metrics on human 
audio-not gTTS and statistically supported improvement or documented accuracy -latency trade-offs 
per language. Until Table 4.2a is populated, H1 remains partially supported (English synthetic subset 
only).

Chapter 5: Discussion 
5.1 Interpretation of ASR Findings 
The English WER mean of Whisper medium 0.0410 was the best observed mean, only half of 
Google’s baseline mean of 0.0806. Its mean latency was over four times Google's mean and over 19.5 
times the tiny fine-tuned model. This proves how wrong choosing a model based on WER alone would 
be in a conversational scenario. It means a smaller model can give better conversation latency, while 
a bigger one would do for asynchronous transcription.  Fine tuning on the tiny model did not reduce 
WER compared to the original tiny model in this test.  The reasons may be the low or synthetic data 
used to fine tune, insufficient number of training steps, differences in training and evaluation speech 
data, or even catastrophic specialization. This test does not  prove that fine -tuning in general is 
pointless. On the contrary, it tells us that the current training setup needs a bigger, more accurate data 
set with proper ablation study. No conclusions about Tamil or Sinhala ASR can be drawn from this 
thesis. The benchmark and fine-tuning pipeline are completed, and public data sets for adaptation of 
Tamil and Sinhala were  located [11], [12], but multilingual voice inp ut was never reached in the 
current deployment. This constraint does not affect the English voice -to-chatbot channel, which 
remains the main one used. 
5.2 Interpretation of Chatbot Findings 
The F1 score for the intent is 0.904 and for the entity is 0.953. These numbers show that a simple 
Rasa pipeline can do a job with common academic tasks. It does not need to send every question to a 
cloud model. This is good for privacy. It helps the system behave in a predictable way. It also makes 
it easier to work with systems that use structured APIs. The result shows that using a framework was 
a good idea. 
If we look at the overall performance we can see some weaknesses. The `out_of_scope` score was 
lower, than the target of 0.85. And the `course_name` extraction was not very good it only got 0.500. 
This is a problem because the system gets confused between appointment and availability questions. 
They use words. So, the system should ask for clarification of deciding when it is not sure. The system 
also got 100% on the test set. This might be because the test set was too easy or had similar patterns. 
In the future we should get a set of user questions after the model is developed.

5.3 Interpretation of Translation Findings 
Zero-error completion in the translation syste m indicates engineering reliability for the current run, 
although the output quality differs significantly depending on the translation direction. The automated 
similarity measure overstates quality in some language pairs, while human ratings (mean 3.76/5. 0) 
indicate usability, albeit mediocre, but do not support using Sinhala to Tamil and other poor -
performing directions in practical settings. Low BLEU score and high similarity do not guarantee 
good translation. Embedding similarity might encourage topic s imilarity even if an incorrect time, 
negation, room number, or lecturer's name is translated. These errors are particularly significant from 
the point of view of the logistical processes within the university. Regarding the developed platform, 
the English language still remains the language of the use interface. Until dedicated low -resource 
models, training on particular language pairs, and larger verified corpus are included into the future 
research efforts, the translation in Tamil and Sinhala must be con sidered experimental [6], [7], [11], 
[12]. 
5.4 Integrated Artifact Contribution 
The thing that really stands out is how language interfaces and the way institutions operate are con-
nected. The chatbot can use things like timetables and navigation services. It can also take speech 
input. Use it to figure out what you want. This can be really helpful for things, like content that needs 
to be translated. You can even use QR codes and graphs to show the way to a place you want to go. 
This way of doing things will make the artificial intelligence system more useful. It will not just be a

to fix mistakes and test it. There are a couple of ways it can figure out the route one is called A* and 
the other is called Dijkstra. 
5.5 Practical Implications 
For deployment, a hybrid system architecture is best suited. Local deployment of Rasa and local 
translation/ASR systems help in better management and enable the system to function without having 
to send each query to a thi rd party. The cloud-based systems can be considered as optional backups 
when consent and policy allow. Selection of models needs to be configurable according to language, 
device capability, network condition, and level of criticality of the task.

5.6 Limitations and Threats to Validity 
Limitations of the current study include: 
1.Valid ASR benchmark is currently available only for the English language; ASR benchmarking and 
fine-tuning for Tamil and Sinhala will be a future Endeavor. 
2.ASR audio data is currently available for a single person and consists of samples/synthetic audio. 
3.There is a need for hardware, warmup time, cache, and network control. 
4.The NLU dataset was authored within the project and may not represent spontaneous user language. 
5.The held -out NLU result may be inflated b y pattern similarity or leakage and requires an 
independent audit. 
6. Tamil/Sinhala translation quality is insufficient for reliable academic use in several directions. 
7.Cloud translation baselines could not be executed because of API credential/rate-limit issues. 
8.BLEU tokenization may be unsuitable for the tested short Tamil and Sinhala sentences. 
9.The usability study used student questionnaires only (n=20); lecturer and administrator cohorts were 
outside this evaluation scope. 
10.The artifact was developed for one university context, limiting institutional generalization. 
11.Floor-plan OCR and graph accuracy have not been reported across a sufficiently diverse building 
set.

Chapter 6: Conclusions 
6.1 Summary of the Research 
This research designed and implemented LECSTU, an AI-integrated academic platform for university 
environments, deployed at https://lecstu.com. The artifact consolidates timetables, staff and hall 
availability, appointments, notifications, outdoor and indoor navigation with QR positioning, English 
speech recognition, conversational assistance through Rasa, and an optional translation layer. It uses 
a responsive React frontend, an Express/PostgreSQL backend, Rasa, and independently deployable 
Python AI microservices. 
6.2 Alignment with Objectives 
RO-1 was partially met: The ASR pipeline, dataset architecture, English baseline, analysis, and 
finetuned engine were implemented and deployed. Evaluation of Tamil/Sinhala ASR and multi -
speaker validation are future work, with finetuning datasets documented (SLR127, SLR52). 
 
RO-2 was met for the defined dataset: The Rasa chatbot was trained and tested, surpassing the F1 
acceptance threshold and revealing actionable errors. Custom actions map intents to real -time 
timetable, appointment, availability, and navigation APIs. 
 
RO-3 was partially met:  The translation service, corpus, automated baseline, and human evaluation 
were performed. Quality of Tamil/Sinhala output is inconsistent and not deemed ready for production; 
cloud performance benchmarking is deferred. 
 
RO-4 was met: Twenty student usability surveys were obtained (SUS average 60.53, SD 14.45; core 
satisfaction 4.7-4.9/5; voice/translation ~3.4/5; 20/20 recommend; T7 averages 4.63-4.79/5). 
 
RO-5 was largely met: A production-focused platform combines academic logistics and AI services, 
is publicly deployed, and features automated tests for several key workflows and hosting 
documentation. 
6.3 Conclusions Drawn from Current Evidence 
The study shows that a domain-specific and modular AI-based architecture can support integration of 
voice and conversational interfaces with university services. Rasa demonstrates adequate intent and

entity recognition performance within the tested academic domain. The proposed platform with its 
timetables, appointments, notifications, and indoor navigation is the main engineering contribution of 
the paper. English ASR technology exhibits an important trade-off between accuracy and latency. The 
most accurate model of ASR technology among tested also demonstrated t he worst latency. Tamil 
and Sinhala speech and translation technology exist but did not achieve appropriate quality in the 
system implementation. The twenty student responses demonstrate the adequacy of perceived 
usability and satisfaction with core features (SUS score of 60.53). 
 
6.4 Closing Remarks 
 
Recommendations for the faculty, system maintainers, and further research are provided in Chapter 
7. These recommendations come from the limitations found in section 5.6, along with the partial 
completion of RO-1 and RO-3. 
6.5 Final Statement 
This chapter illustrates the application of AI to create accountability as a user interface for university 
services, and not applying it as an isolated conversational tool. The major benefit of this work lies in 
the capabi lity of utilizing structured data from academics, voice and chatbot communication in 
English language, as well as indoor navigation in a replicable and deployable research project. Support 
in Tamil and Sinhala languages is another area for research and dev elopment, based on the existing 
literature of low-resource languages. Chapter 7 describes how this will be beneficial.
