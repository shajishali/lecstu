Abstract 
The increasing scale of customer interactions in the e -commerce sector has created a critical 
demand for automated, intelligent Customer Relationship Management (CRM) systems. While 
proprietary Large Language Models (LLMs) such as GPT -4 perform well in such tasks, their 
high costs make them inaccessible to many small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs). Open-
source alternatives like LLaMA -3.1 offer a more affordable solution, but face limitations in 
accuracy and contextual reasoning when applied to complex CRM tasks. This study 
investigates whether these limitations can be overcome using a multi-agent system. 
The aim of the research is to optimize open-source LLMs for CRM automation in e-commerce 
by using task -specific agents to improve performance in three key tasks: Named Entity 
Disambiguation (NED), Policy Violation Identification (PVI), and Knowledge-based Question 
Answering (KQA). The primary objective was to design and evaluate a scalable framework 
that enhances the accuracy and reliability of open -source LLMs in automating these three 
critical CRM tasks.  The CRMArena benchmark, developed by Salesforce AI Research, was 
used as the test environment, simulating realistic CRM scenarios across 130 queries per task. 
Two model variants, LLaMA -3.1 70B and 405B, were tested using LangGraph -based multi-
agent workflows. The 70B model achieved 92.31% accuracy in NED , 59.23% in PVI , and 
85.38% in KQA, while the 405B model reached 86.92% (NED), 36.15% (PVI), and 84.62% 
(KQA). These results outperformed the CRMArena benchmark scores for open-source models 
typically under 55%, showing that multi -agent task decomposition significantly enhances 
model effectiveness. The solution was also cost-efficient, with per-task inference costs as low 
as $0.1 
The key conclusion of this study is that a well-structured multi-agent system can substantially 
improve the performance of open-source LLMs in CRM scenarios, enabling cost-effective and 
accurate automation comparable to that of proprietary solutions. Moreo ver, the architecture 
proved modular, interpretable, and adaptable, allowing each agent to specialize in specific 
subtasks and improving overall reasoning and reliability. 
As a recommendation for future work, this framework could be further enhanced through 
domain-specific fine-tuning, integration of memory and feedback loops, and deployment in 
real-world CRM environments to validate its robustness under live customer interaction data.

Chapter 1 
Introduction 
1.1 Introduction 
The e -commerce sector has experienced rapid growth in recent years, driven by increased 
consumer reliance on online shopping platforms. According to Statista [1], global retail e -
commerce sales have shown a consistent upward trend, highlighting the need for more robust 
digital infrastructure. This surge has intensified the demand for effective Customer 
Relationship Management (CRM) systems capable of managing large volumes of custom er 
interactions, delivering personalized experiences, and maintaining adherence to business 
policies. Traditional CRM systems, often dependent on manual processes, struggle to keep up 
with this expansion, leading to inefficiencies, higher costs, and lower customer satisfaction [2]. 
Artificial Intelligence (AI), particularly Large Language Models (LLMs), offers great potential 
to automate CRM tasks, streamlining operations, improving personalization, and enabling 
data-driven decisions [2]. 
However, open-source LLMs, such as LLaMA-3.1, face significant challenges in e-commerce 
CRM automation. The CRMArena benchmark  [3] indicates that these models achieve 
suboptimal performance, with success rates below 55% in critical tasks like Knowledge 
Question Answering (KQA), Named Entity Disambiguation (NED), and Policy Violation 
Identification (PVI). These limitations arise from difficulties in understanding nuanced 
contexts, executing multi-step tasks, and integrating with CRM system interfaces. In contrast, 
proprietary models offer higher accuracy but come with prohibitive costs, making them  
inaccessible for many e -commerce businesses, especially startups and small -to-medium 
enterprises (SMEs). This performance gap highlights a critical need for cost-effective, reliable 
open-source LLM solutions tailored for professional CRM tasks in e-commerce [3]. 
1.2 Research Problem 
In the e-commerce industry, Customer Relationship Management (CRM) systems are vital for 
managing customer interactions, resolving order issues, and answering product -related 
questions. Large Language Models (LLMs) show promise in automating these tasks, but open-
source LLMs, such as LLaMA -3.1, face significant challenges in achieving the required

• Knowledge Question Answering (KQA) : Providing accurate, policy -compliant

on KQA, NED, and PVI, through a comprehensive literature review.

specific agents and workflow decomposition to address contextual and multi -step 
challenges. 
3. Implement the framework in a simulated e-commerce environment, testing its 
performance on 130 diverse CRM task queries to achieve a target accuracy exceeding 
55%. 
4. Compare the performance of optimized models against existing open -source and 
proprietary models using the CRMArena benchmark.

2.3 LLMs in CRM Automation 
Large language models (LLMs) have become essential tools for streamlining customer 
relationship management (CRM) tasks in the e-commerce world, promising to boost both 
operational efficiency and customer satisfaction. Yet, when it comes to implementing these 
models—especially open -source ones like LLaMA -3.1—there are notable hurdles to 
overcome, primarily due to their struggles with accura cy and contextual understanding in the 
messy reality of real-world situations [3]. 
2.3.1 LLMs in Automating CRM Tasks 
The CRMArena benchmark  [3] shows that LLM -based agents are set to transform CRM by 
taking care of everyday tasks like answering customer questions, spotting policy violations, 
and sorting out order problems. This not only boosts efficiency but also enhances customer 
satisfaction. For example, LLMs can understand customer inquiries, pull out key information, 
and deliver precise answers by tapping into external data sources like product catalogs or policy 
databases [3]. 
The ReAct framework brings together reasoning and action, boosting the capabilities of LLMs 
to tackle various tasks by allowing for step -by-step planning and interaction with APIs. This 
was clearly shown in settings like WebShop, where it surpassed traditi onal methods [8]. 
Likewise, benchmarks such as WorkArena illustrate how LLMs can engage with enterprise 
software (like ServiceNow) to carry out tasks such as updating customer records or generating 
reports, effectively mimicking the work of knowledge workers [8]. 
Beyond just automating tasks, LLMs are transforming how businesses interact with customers 
by sifting through vast amounts of data to anticipate their needs and customize responses. In 
realistic online settings like WebArena  [9], LLMs can seamlessly navigate e-commerce sites 
to help with various shopping activities, whether it’s searching for products or tracking orders, 
all through natural language commands. AssistantBench [10] showcases how LLMs can tackle 
tedious tasks like drafting emails or pulling up customer details, which are essential for 
effective CRM.  
These features are perfectly in tune with the e-commerce sector's demand for round-the-clock 
support and insights driven by data, as LLMs can work alongside CRM tools to create a 
comprehensive view of customers and offer proactive assistance. However, putting LLMs into

comes to open-source models. 
2.3.2 Challenges and Limitations of LLMs in CRM 
While LLMs have a lot of potential, they still struggle to deliver consistent performance in 
CRM tasks, mainly because real -world situations can be quite complex. The CRMArena 
benchmark [3] shows that even the most advanced LLM agents manage to succeed in fewer 
than 40% of tasks when using ReAct prompting, and only about 55% with specially designed 
function-calling tools. This highlights a significant reliability gap. The low success rate is  
largely due to challenges in grasping nuanced contexts, managing multi -step tasks, and 
effectively interacting with CRM system APIs. For instance, tasks like Named Entity 
Disamiguation (NED) require accurate entity recognition, while Policy Violation Identification 
(PVI) involves navigating intricate policy frameworks —both of which put a strain on LLMs' 
ability to reason contextually. 
Other benchmarks back up these findings. WorkBench [11], which evaluates AI agents on 690 
workplace tasks, reveals that even the top -performing model (GPT-4) only hits a success rate 
of 43%. In contrast, open -source models like Llama2 -70B barely scrape by at just 3%. The 
errors range from using the wrong tools to making serious mistakes, like sending emails to the 
wrong person, which really raises concerns about reliability. Similarly, WebArena [9] indicates 
that GPT-4-based agents manage to complete only 14.41% of realistic web tasks, while humans 
achieve a much higher rate of 78.24%. This gap is largely due to the AI's difficulties with 
dynamic web environments and multi -step planning. AssistantBench [10] also points out that 
no model surpasses 26% accuracy on complex web tasks, facing challenges like hallucination 
and poor navigation on the web. These issues become even more pronounced in software -
related tasks, as shown in SWE -bench [12], where Claude 2 only resolves 1.96% of GitHub 
issues, highlighting the limitations of LLMs when it comes to tackling intricate, context -
sensitive problems[9]. 
2.3.3 Gaps in Open-Source LLMs 
The performance gaps are especially noticeable when it comes to open -source LLMs, which 
tend to fall short compared to proprietary models in CRM automation. According to 
CRMArena’s findings [3], open-source models like LLaMA-3.1 have a tough time hitting the 
accuracy levels needed for professional CRM tasks, with their success rates trailing behind 
proprietary options like GPT-4 or Claude. WorkArena [8] points out a significant performance

gap, highlighting that open-source models often underperform in enterprise software tasks due 
to their limited reasoning and tool -use capabilities. This disparity is particularly crucial for e-
commerce, where achieving high accuracy in tasks like knowledge question answering (KQA) 
or policy violation identification (PVI) is vital for maintaining customer trust and compliance. 
The absence of solid, real-world benchmarks for CRM has really held back the assessment and 
enhancement of open-source LLMs[3]. CRMArena [3] tries to tackle this issue by simulating

2.4 Multi-Agent Frameworks in CRM 
Multi-agent systems (MAS) have emerged as a promising approach to address the limitations 
of open-source LLMs in Customer Relationship Management (CRM) automation, as evidenced 
by benchmarks like CRMArena [3]. These systems leverage specialized agents to enhance task 
efficiency and personalization in e-commerce CRM. 
Studies like Wang et al.  [13]  suggest that MAS enhance CRM tasks, such as Knowledge -
augmented Question Answering, by distributing subtasks across specialized agents, potentially 
improving accuracy over single-model LLMs. For example, task decomposition allows agents 
to focus on specific functions, such as query analysis or data retrieval, which may improve 
performance in complex CRM scenarios. 
Evaluations from WorkArena and WebArena [8], [9] demonstrate that MAS excel in web -
based CRM tasks by assigning agents to functions like data scraping or compliance checks. 
These systems outperform monolithic LLMs in contextual understanding and multi -step 
reasoning, addressing performance gaps in open-source models. 
Thakkar and Yadav [14] propose a multimodal MAS for personalized e-commerce 
recommendations, comprising three agents: a Product Recommendation Agent, a Multimodal

Recommendation Agent, and a Market Analysis Agent. Using LLMs like Gemini-1.5-pro and 
LLaMA-70B, the system integrates text, images, and real-time market trends to deliver tailored 
suggestions. The Product Recommendation Agent analyses user queries, the Multimodal Agent 
processes images for visual -based recommendations, and the Market Analysis Agent aligns 
suggestions with market trends. Evaluated on metrics like Precision  at K, Recall at K, and 
ROUGE, LLaMA -70B achieved a Recall  at K of 1.0 and NDCG of 1.0  for product 
recommendations, while Gemma2-9b-it scored a ROUGE-1 F-score of 0.5042 for market trend 
analysis [14]. This multimodal approach enhances recommendation accuracy compared to text-
only systems.

3.4 Design 
This section presents the design of a modular, intelligent system to automate complex 
Customer Relationship Management (CRM) tasks —Named Entity Disambiguation (NED), 
Policy Violation Identification (PVI), and Knowledge -based Question Answering (KQA) —
within the CRMArena sandbox [3]. The system leverages a multi -agent architecture 
orchestrated by LangGraph to ensure flexibility, scalability, and task-specific processing. 
3.4.1 Objective of the Design 
The design aims to: 
• Implement a Task Classifier to route queries to the appropriate workflow automatically. 
• Develop task-specific agent workflows for NED, PVI, and KQA to handle complex, 
multi-step CRM queries. 

===== PAGE 28 =====

28 
 
• Optimize prompt engineering  and agent colla boration to improved accuracy and 
efficiency. 
3.4.2 System Architecture 
The system employs a multi -agent graph -based architecture orchestrated by LangGraph, 
ensuring modularity and scalability. Key components include: 
• Task Classifier Agent : Identifies the task type (NED, PVI, KQA) based on query 
structure and keywords, routing the query to the corresponding task agent using a 
prompt-based LLM classifier. 
• Task Agents : Specialized agents for NED, PVI, and KQA, each with sub -agents 
handling individual steps of the task. 
• Query Tools: Utilize Salesforce Object Query Language (SOQL) for structured data 
retrieval and Salesforce Object Search Language (SOSL) for text-based searches. 
• State Management: Each agent maintains its own WorkflowState to track query data, 
intermediate results, and errors specific to its workflow, ensuring isolated and task -
specific state handling. 
3.4.2.1 Rationale for Design Choices 
The architecture leverages a multi-agent approach and LangGraph for the following reasons: 
• Multi-Agent Approach : By assigning distinct agents to NED, PVI, and KQA, the 
system modularizes task-specific logic, improving reusability and debugging compared 
to a monolithic system. This approach supports CRMArena’s complex, multi -step 
query requirements [3]. 
• LangGraph: Chosen for its low -level flexibility, LangGraph enables explicit control 
over state transitions, conditional routing, and retry logic. Unlike other frameworks 
(e.g., CrewAI, AutoGen), it provides transparent debugging, separate state handling for 
each agent, and graph -based workflows, making it ideal for orchestrating multi -step 
CRM tasks.

Discussion 
5.1 Introduction 
This section interprets the findings from the multi -agent system (MAS) evaluation on the 
CRMArena dataset [3] for Named Entity Disambiguation (NED), Policy Violation 
Identification (PVI), and Knowledge-based Question Answering (KQA) tasks, as presented in 
Chapter 4. It discusses the MAS’s performance, inference cost implications, semantic accuracy 
of KQA responses, and a comparison with prior studies, while addressing limitations in the 
research methodology to provide a comprehensive evaluation of the proje ct’s outcomes for e-
commerce Customer Relationship Management (CRM) automation. 
5.2 Interpretation of Performance Across Tasks 
The results in Chapter 4, Table 3: Accuracy and Inference Cost for NED, PVI, and KQA ) 
demonstrate varied MAS performance across NED, PVI, and KQA tasks using LLaMA -3.1 
models (70B and 405B). The 70B model outperformed the 405B model in NED, achieving 
92.31% accuracy (120/130 queries) compared to 86.92% (113/130), likely due to NED’s 
reliance on straightforward object mappings. This suggests that the 70B model efficiently 
processed the CRMArena dataset’s structured data for product identification tasks.  
In contrast, PVI exhibited lower accuracies of 59.23% (70B, 77/130) and 36.15% (405B, 
47/130), indicating challenges in detecting policy violations. The complexity of matching Case 
records to Knowledge__kav articles may have hindered performance, requiring  nuanced 
reasoning that both models struggled to achieve.  
KQA showed robust performance, with accuracies of 85.38% (70B, 111/130) and 84.62% 
(405B, 110/130) based on a BERTScore F1 threshold of ≥ 0.85, reflecting high semantic 
accuracy for open-ended queries.

5.3 Inference Cost Implications 
In Chapter 4 , Table 3: Accuracy and Inference Cost for NED, PVI, and KQA ) reveals

especially for tasks involving extensive text generation. These cost -performance trade -offs 
inform practical decisions for CRM automation, favouring the 70B model for cost -sensitive 
applications.

5.6 Research Limitations 
The research methodology has several limitations that impact the findings and their 
generalizability, as evaluated below: 
1. Limited Model Diversity: The study used only LLaMA -3.1 models (70B and 405B), 
excluding other advanced LLMs (e.g., GPT-4, used in CRMArena [3] and WorkArena 
[8]). This restricts comparisons with broader LLM capabilities, potentially 
underestimating the MAS’s performance with more advanced models. 
2. Narrow Task Scope: The evaluation focused on three CRMArena tasks (NED, PVI, 
KQA), representing Service Agent duties, limiting insights into other CRM roles (e.g., 
Service Manager, Service Analyst) or broader enterprise tasks. 

===== PAGE 48 =====

3. Synthetic Dataset Constraints : The CRMArena dataset [3], a synthetic model of a 
mid-sized shoe company ( schema provided in Appendix A), may not fully represent 
larger or dynamic CRM systems, potentially limiting the MAS’s applicability. 
4. Complex Reasoning Challenges: The lower PVI accuracy (59.23% for 70B) indicates 
limitations in tasks requiring intricate reasoning, such as navigating complex object 
dependencies (e.g., Case to Knowledge__kav, Appendix B, Figure 13: CRMArena 
Sandbox Schema Diagram). 
5. Inference Cost Scalability: High inference costs for the 405B model (e.g., $0.85 for 
KQA) suggest scalability challenges for resource -constrained environments, with no 
exploration of optimization techniques (e.g., model quantization). 
These limitations suggest that while the MAS achieved high accuracy within the study’s scope, 
its effectiveness in diverse, real-world CRM contexts requires further validation.

Recommendations 
7.1 Enhancing PVI Performance 
The MAS achieved a lower accuracy for PVI, indicating challenges in complex reasoning tasks 
involving intricate object. To improve PVI performance, the following actions are 
recommended: 
• Develop Advanced Agent Coordination : Implement specialized PVI agents with 
enhanced reasoning capabilities, such as chaining multiple reasoning steps using 
LangGraph, to improve policy violation detection accuracy. 
• Fine-Tune Models for PVI : Fine -tune LLaMA -3.1 models on a curated subset of 
CRMArena’s Knowledge__kav articles and Case records to better handle policy -
specific queries, reducing errors in complex scenarios. 
• Incorporate Rule-Based Heuristics: Integrate rule-based systems alongside LLMs to 
enforce strict policy compliance checks, complementing the MAS’s data -driven 
approach. 
7.2 Optimizing Inference Cost Scalability 
The high inference costs of the 405B model (e.g., $0.85 for KQA, Table 3  in Chapter 4 ) 
highlight scalability challenges for resource-constrained SMEs. To enhance cost-effectiveness, 
the following actions are recommended: 
• Explore Model Optimization Techniques : Apply model quantization or pruning to 
reduce the computational demands of the 405B model, lowering inference costs while 
maintaining accuracy. 
• Batch Processing for Queries : Implement batch processing for CRM queries to 
optimize API calls, reducing costs for high-volume tasks like KQA. 
• Hybrid Model Deployment : Deploy the 70B model for cost -sensitive tasks (e.g., 
NED, $0.04) and reserve the 405B model for high -stakes tasks requiring nuanced 
responses, balancing cost and performance. 
These strategies would make the MAS more scalable, supporting its adoption in SME e -
commerce environments.

You are a Salesforce Task Classifier Agent in a CRM automation system. 
Your job is to classify user queries into one of the following three 
categories based on their intent and wording: 
 
1. NED (Named Entity Disambiguation) 
These queries refer to specific products that the customer has already 
purchased. 
They often include time filters or product descriptions. 
Examples: 
- "Display the waterproof hiking boots I purchased three days back" 
- "Can you display the adjustable weight dumbbells I purchased a month ago?" 
- "Can you display the women's trail jacket I purchased a fortnight ago?" 
 
2. PVI (Policy Violation Identification) 
These queries question whether an agent followed company policy during a 
specific interaction or case. 
Examples: 
- "Did the agent breach the policy in this situation?" 
- "Did the agent breach the policy in this instance?" 
- "Did the agent breach the policy?" 
 
3. KQA (Knowledge Question Answering) 
These are general knowledge-based questions not tied to a specific purchase 
or agent behavior. 
They often relate to product features, company policies, or usage 
instructions. 
Examples: 
- "What technology features do Shoes & Clothings golf shoes have?" 
- "What is the time frame for being eligible for a full refund with Shoes & 
Clothings?" 
- "How can I store my yoga straps to prolong their life?" 
 
Now classify the following user query into **only one** of the above 
categories. 
User Query: "{query}"

Respond with exactly one word: NED, PVI, or KQA. 
"""
